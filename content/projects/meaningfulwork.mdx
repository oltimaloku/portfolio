## The problem

For a small nonprofit, its most important relationships (sponsors, major donors, partners, new members) don't live in a CRM. They live in someone's inbox. CRMs ask busy staff to copy everything somewhere else, so they don't, and relationships quietly go cold between emails.

I started by talking to the people with the problem: more than fifteen interviews with nonprofit teams about where relationships fell through the cracks. The answer was consistent. The inbox was never designed to be a CRM, but it's where the work already happens. So the product should meet them there.

## Key Relationships

[MeaningfulWork](https://www.meaningful.ca) is an operating system for nonprofits: donations, volunteers, campaigns and members. I designed and built Key Relationships as a lightweight CRM on top of email inside it, in two views.

### The inbox view

A curated inbox for the contacts that matter, with *All*, *Important* and *Done* filters, so it can be worked to zero like a to-do list. Any email can become a key relationship in one click. A contact sidebar holds each relationship's value, tags and tasks, and there's a Gmail-style compose window, threaded replies, HTML rendering and attachment preview, so day-to-day email doesn't have to leave the product.

### The flow view

Every relationship moves through a **flow**: a pipeline of stages such as *research, cultivation, solicitation, stewardship* for fundraising. Flows appear as a drag-and-drop Kanban board or a list. Each one tracks its own goal, conversion rate, leads still needed and stage-by-stage conversion, so a team can see at a glance where relationships stall.

## How it's built

- **Email sync with Nylas.** OAuth2 connection with state verification, several email accounts per organisation, inbox and sent folders, sending, attachments, and errors handled by category (expired auth, provider outage, rate limits) with retries.
- **Metadata first, bodies on demand.** Instead of copying every email, the backend stores only metadata and fetches full bodies when they're opened, with a cache. Sync stops early once most fetched emails are already known, and a background job running every 30 minutes gave way to an on-demand refresh.
- **Pipelines that survive editing.** Flow and relationship models, services and routes on the backend, written so stage IDs stay stable when a team renames or reorders its stages.
- **A front end that stays fast.** One central data hook, SWR caching (including tracking down an infinite refresh loop), restoring state per session with loading in the background, and pagination as you scroll.

Within the feature, I built almost all of it, front end and back end, including its API.

## Working with pilot clients

I prototyped quickly with v0, then replaced each placeholder with the real thing. The product went to two pilot organisations through four or five rounds of client feedback, and each round reshaped what got built next.

## What I learned

This was the clearest case I've had of product work and engineering being one job. The interviews decided the shape of the product (put it in the inbox), and the shape of the product decided the hardest engineering problems: syncing email reliably, cheaply and fast.
