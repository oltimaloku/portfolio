## Introduction

Have you ever thought you understood something, only to find yourself stumbling when trying to explain it to someone else? This common experience reveals a fundamental truth about learning: there's a vast difference between passive familiarity and genuine understanding.

The Feynman Technique, named after the Nobel Prize-winning physicist Richard Feynman, addresses this gap head-on. It's a learning method that forces you to confront what you truly know versus what you only think you know.

![Richard Feynman teaching](/images/articles/feynman-teaching.webp)

## The Four Steps

The technique is elegantly simple, consisting of just four steps:

### 1. Choose a Concept

Select the topic you want to understand. Write the name of the concept at the top of a blank page. This could be anything from a programming concept like "recursion" to a physics principle like "entropy."

### 2. Teach It to a Child

Write an explanation of the concept as if you were teaching it to a 12-year-old. This forces you to use simple language and avoid jargon. If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough.

> "If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough." — Often attributed to Einstein

### 3. Identify Gaps and Go Back to the Source

When you get stuck or find yourself using complex terms, you've identified a gap in your understanding. Go back to your source material and relearn these areas until you can explain them in simple terms.

### 4. Review and Simplify

Review your explanation, looking for areas that are still too complex or confusing. Use analogies and simple examples to make the concept more accessible.

![The learning cycle](/images/articles/learning-cycle.webp)

## Why It Works

The Feynman Technique is effective for several psychological reasons:

**Active Recall**: Instead of passively reading, you're actively reconstructing knowledge from memory. This strengthens neural pathways.

**Elaborative Interrogation**: By forcing yourself to explain "why" and "how," you create deeper connections with existing knowledge.

**Metacognition**: The process makes you aware of what you know and don't know, allowing targeted learning.

## Applying It to Programming

As a developer, I've found this technique invaluable. Here's an example of how I might use it to understand closures in JavaScript:

**Attempt 1 (too complex):**
"A closure is a function that retains access to its lexical scope even when executed outside of it."

**Attempt 2 (simplified):**
"Imagine a function is like a backpack. When you create a function inside another function, the inner function packs up all the variables it can see into its backpack. Even when it travels far away from where it was created, it still has access to everything in its backpack."

The second explanation uses a concrete analogy that a child could understand, which means I truly grasp the concept.

## Practical Tips

Here are some tips for getting the most out of the Feynman Technique:

- **Use pen and paper**: Writing by hand slows you down and forces clearer thinking
- **Actually speak out loud**: Verbalizing engages different cognitive processes
- **Don't skip the hard parts**: The areas where you struggle are exactly where you need the most work
- **Use analogies liberally**: Good analogies are signs of deep understanding
- **Revisit regularly**: Spaced repetition combined with this technique is powerful

## Conclusion

The Feynman Technique isn't just a study hack—it's a philosophy of learning. It embodies the idea that true understanding means being able to communicate clearly. In a world full of jargon and complexity, this simple approach cuts through the noise.

Whether you're learning to code, studying for exams, or trying to master a new skill, give this technique a try. You might be surprised by how much you don't know—and how quickly that can change.
